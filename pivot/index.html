Pivot

A budget-first meal planner for students, designed for the night the plan falls apart.

Problem

Meal planners assume a perfect week. Students buy for 7 days, cook 2, order takeout, and throw the rest out.

Bet

Plan less and plan for failure: 3 shared-ingredient meals, a budget cap, and takeout as part of the plan.

Built

An end-to-end prototype with Sunday planning, a priced grocery list and a way out on tired nights. Peer-reviewed at 4.89/5.

04 · Core loop

Pick 3 → Shop → See the week → Cook

01

Pick 3 on Sunday

Instead of filling seven slots, you drag in three meals. Pivot only offers recipes that share ingredients, so the basket stays under your weekly budget.

02

One smart shop

Three recipes become one priced list. Pivot shows its work as it builds the list, so you can trust the result:

  • Reads your 3 recipes
  • Merges shared ingredients
  • Skips what’s already in your fridge
  • Prices the basket
03

A week with a night off

Takeout night is a planned slot in the week, so ordering food is part of the plan, not a broken streak. Meals that need cooking go around it.

04

Cook today

The Today view shows one next meal and your progress (“Meal 1 of 3”). The Pivot slider trades budget against nutrition, so you can swap in a higher-protein version and see what it costs.

05 · The pivot moment

When Wednesday happens

This is where the product gets its name. When you’re too tired to cook the plan, you get two ways out that still use what you already bought.

06 · Key decisions

What I changed, and why

Grocery list handoff
Before

Close the modal and return home

After

Land on the cart, so users see the result right away

The first flow worked, but after the modal closed nothing on screen showed that a list had been made. Landing on the cart makes the result visible. This is where “logic isn’t experience” came from.

The 3-meal rule
Before

A rule that enforces the budget

After

A flexible starting point, not a rule

A hard limit would bring back the guilt Pivot is meant to remove. Three meals is now the default, and you can change it.

07 · Supporting features

Less typing, fewer surprises

These support the core loop. Each one removes a step that would otherwise make students give up on planning.

10 · Heuristic review

Auditing my own prototype

Peers rated the flows highly. Then I checked the recorded prototype against Nielsen’s 10 usability heuristics myself, rating each issue from 1 (cosmetic) to 4 (must fix). Four issues scored a 3. Below are the two that matter most, before and after.

Other issues and fixes
3Two shopping lists. Prep and Week each show a list with a different total. → Keep one list. Week links to it.Consistency
3Receipt scan writes straight to the fridge. → Add a review step before saving, plus a quick fridge check before Sunday planning.Error prevention
2Three “what do I eat” buttons on one screen. → One “Too tired?” entry that opens both rescue options.Minimalism
2Budget reads $180, $30 and $127 in one flow. → One budget model shown the same way everywhere.Consistency
2Six onboarding steps, including a visual style picker. → Replace it with diet and fitness goals, the thing peers asked for.Minimalism
2Meals can only be dragged into slots. → Tap a meal to fill the next empty slot.Flexibility

What already worked: the “Building your list” progress steps, the “Used in” thumbnails on every grocery item, missing-ingredient prices, back and close on every step, and a Generate button that stays disabled until 3 meals are picked.

11 · What I learned

Two lessons, one next step

Logic isn’t experience

A flow can be correct and still leave people unsure it worked. If people can’t see the result, the feature doesn’t feel like it worked.

Explore wide, then simplify

Generating ideas is cheap. Choosing what to cut is the design work.

Next: plans that know you

All three reviewers pointed to the same gap. Add dietary restrictions and fitness goals to onboarding, so budget, nutrition and the Wednesday rescue all work from one profile.
